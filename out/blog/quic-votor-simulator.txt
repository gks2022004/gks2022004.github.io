16:T2e44,<p>Let me tell you about the time I read a four-bullet GitHub issue and somehow ended up
writing a distributed-systems prototype at 1 AM, fully convinced I was about to become
a Firedancer contributor.</p>
<p>Spoiler: I did not become a Firedancer contributor.</p>
<p>But I <em>did</em> get a really good story, a working simulator, and a brief, character-building
encounter with the word "Sorry."</p>
<h2>How It Started (Innocently)</h2>
<p>Firedancer issue <a href="https://github.com/firedancer-io/firedancer/issues/10307"><code>#10307</code></a>: <strong>votor QUIC support</strong>.</p>
<p>Short issue. Dangerous issue. The kind of issue that looks like a snack and turns out to
be a five-course meal with a wine pairing you didn't order.</p>
<p>The bullets read like a polite list of reasons your weekend is now gone:</p>
<ul>
<li>Alpenglow Votor needs all-to-all communication over QUIC. (Cool, cool.)</li>
<li>Firedancer needs scalable QUIC client support. (Sure, scalable, my favorite word.)</li>
<li>It needs scalable outgoing uni-stream support. (Now we're just adding adjectives.)</li>
<li>It might need stream fragment reassembly because Agave doesn't guarantee votes arrive
unfragmented. (Ah. There it is. The trap.)</li>
<li>QUIC datagrams would've made this easy, but they're not available for launch. (Of course
they aren't. That would be <em>nice</em>.)</li>
</ul>
<p>I read this and thought, with the unearned confidence of a person who has not yet suffered:
"I should model this."</p>
<h2>The Part Where I Convince Myself This Is Reasonable</h2>
<p>Here's the thing about QUIC streams. Datagrams are message-shaped — you send a thing, it
arrives as a thing. Lovely. Civilized.</p>
<p>Streams are <em>byte</em> soup. Reliable, ordered byte soup, but soup. Your beautiful consensus
vote can show up as "bytes 0–40, then bytes 90–120, then the middle part, eventually, maybe,
in an order chosen by a malicious goblin." And consensus code does <em>not</em> want soup. It wants
the whole vote, intact, before it does anything important.</p>
<p>So somebody has to stand at the door, catch all the soup, and reassemble it back into a
sandwich.</p>
<p>I decided that somebody was going to be me. In Rust. On a Tuesday.</p>
<h2>The Simulator (a.k.a. My Beautiful Lab Goblin)</h2>
<p>I built a little standalone thing with <code>tokio</code> and <code>quinn</code>:</p>
<p><a href="https://github.com/gks2022004/quic-votor-simulator">https://github.com/gks2022004/quic-votor-simulator</a></p>
<p>Not because Firedancer should use Rust — it absolutely should not, it's a C codebase and I
respect that — but because Rust let me build the state machine fast and the borrow checker
kept me honest about the parts I was tempted to lie about.</p>
<p>It does three things, and I'm a little proud of all of them.</p>
<h3>1. Uni-Stream Fanout (controlled chaos)</h3>
<p>The client splits each vote into fragments and fires each fragment down its own QUIC
uni-stream. And — this is my favorite part — it sends them <strong>in reverse order on purpose</strong>,
because if my reassembler can't survive me actively bullying it, it can't survive the real
network either.</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span>[MUXER] sending vote 3 attempt 1 as 4 uni-stream fragments</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] vote 3 fragment 4/4 on attempt 1</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] vote 3 fragment 3/4 on attempt 1</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] vote 3 fragment 2/4 on attempt 1</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] vote 3 fragment 1/4 on attempt 1</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>Backwards. Like a little villain.</p>
<h3>2. Fragment Reassembly (catching the soup)</h3>
<p>Server side: a map from vote id to a fragment buffer, chunks slotted in by index, and once
<code>0..total</code> are all present it flattens the whole thing back into the original payload.</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] reassembled vote 3: VOTE_3_ACCEPT_BLOCK_C_PTO_DEMO</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>That line gave me a genuinely embarrassing amount of joy. The goblin sandwich was whole again.</p>
<p>In the real Firedancer world the key would be more like <code>(connection, stream_id)</code>, and the
production version would have to be bounded, allocator-aware, and deeply paranoid about
duplicates, overlaps, max message size, stream resets, and cleanup. My version is paranoid
about roughly half of those, which is a polite way of saying it's a prototype.</p>
<h3>3. PTO-Like Retry (where I drop a packet on purpose and then act surprised)</h3>
<p>The client waits for an app-level ACK. And for the demo, I rigged vote 3 so the server
<em>intentionally</em> eats the first ACK:</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] intentionally dropping first ACK for vote 3</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>Sabotaging my own program is, I've decided, an underrated form of testing. The client times
out, shrugs, and retransmits on fresh streams:</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span>[PTO] vote 3 expired; scheduling attempt 2</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[MUXER] sending vote 3 attempt 2 as 4 uni-stream fragments</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>...</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[SERVER] ACK sent for vote 3</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[CLIENT] ACK received for vote 3</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>[CLIENT] all votes ACKed</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>It worked. I felt like a genius.</p>
<p>(Reality check, delivered later, by myself, to myself: real QUIC <em>already</em> has loss recovery
and PTO. Re-implementing it on top is how you end up with two retransmission systems fighting
in a parking lot. The real concern is backpressure and stream-creation failure, not
reinventing the transport. But for a mental model? Chef's kiss.)</p>
<h2>Then I Actually Read the Firedancer Code</h2>
<p>Plot twist: the hard part was already done. Firedancer has a full C QUIC stack in
<code>src/waltz/quic</code>, and the APIs are <em>right there</em>:</p>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="c" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="c" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#6F42C1;--shiki-dark:#B392F0">fd_quic_conn_new_stream</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0">( </span><span style="--shiki-light:#1976D2;--shiki-dark:#79B8FF">fd_quic_conn_t</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> *</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0"> conn );</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span style="--shiki-light:#6F42C1;--shiki-dark:#B392F0">fd_quic_stream_send</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0">( </span><span style="--shiki-light:#1976D2;--shiki-dark:#79B8FF">fd_quic_stream_t</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> *</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0"> stream</span><span style="--shiki-light:#212121;--shiki-dark:#BBBBBB">,</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> void</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> const</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> *</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0"> data</span><span style="--shiki-light:#212121;--shiki-dark:#BBBBBB">,</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0"> ulong data_sz</span><span style="--shiki-light:#212121;--shiki-dark:#BBBBBB">,</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D32F2F;--shiki-dark:#F97583"> int</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292EFF;--shiki-dark:#B392F0"> fin );</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>And the receive callback hands you exactly what a reassembly layer dreams about — <code>stream_id</code>,
<code>offset</code>, <code>data</code>, <code>data_sz</code>, <code>fin</code>. It was like showing up to build a house and finding the
foundation, plumbing, and most of the walls already there.</p>
<p>So the real first contribution wasn't "write QUIC." It was a bounded reassembly + send-path
layer on top, with tests for all the hostile cases: out-of-order chunks, duplicates,
overlaps, <code>FIN</code>-before-complete, max-size rejection. A nice, reviewable little slice.</p>
<p>I wrote all this up. Simulator, analysis, proposed C path. Hit comment. Leaned back. Cracked
the knuckles. Prepared for glory.</p>
<h2>The "No" Heard Round My Apartment</h2>
<figure data-rehype-pretty-code-figure=""><pre tabindex="0" data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark"><code data-language="text" data-theme="min-light min-dark" style="display: grid;"><span data-line=""><span>Sorry, we are not accepting external contributions for this component at the</span></span>
<span data-line=""><span>moment. Thanks for understanding.</span></span></code></pre></figure>
<p>Reader, I was <em>crushed</em> for approximately five seconds.</p>
<p>Then the adult part of my brain rebooted. Of course they said no. Votor transport is
consensus-adjacent, launch-sensitive code. A random internet person showing up with a Rust
prototype and a plan — even a <em>good</em> plan — is not what you want touching the thing that
decides what's real on a blockchain at launch. Maintainers have to protect review bandwidth,
architectural consistency, and the security blast radius. "No" there is just good engineering
management wearing a polite hat.</p>
<p>And crucially, the answer wasn't "you're wrong" or "you don't get it." It was "not here, not
now." That's a boundary, not a grade. The boundary is <em>information</em>.</p>
<h2>What I Actually Walked Away With</h2>
<p>Technically: QUIC streams shove all the complexity to the application boundary. If you can't
have datagrams and your app still thinks in messages, you owe the universe a careful bridge
between byte-stream delivery and message-level logic — bounded memory, stream identity,
offset-aware inserts, dup/overlap handling, <code>FIN</code>-based sizing, cleanup, and tests written by
someone who clearly has trust issues.</p>
<p>Personally: not every rabbit hole has a merged PR at the bottom. Sometimes the prize is that
you turned a four-bullet issue into a working mental model, learned where the real integration
point lives, and got to feel the specific 1 AM joy of watching a backwards-shuffled vote
reassemble itself.</p>
<p>I'd do it again. I will <em>probably</em> do it again. The next issue also looks like a snack.</p>
<h2>Final Thought</h2>
<p>The maintainer said no.</p>
<p><img src="/reply.png" alt="The maintainer&#x27;s reply: &#x22;Sorry, we are not accepting external contributions for this component at the moment. Thanks for understanding.&#x22;"></p>
<p>That's fine. The repo's a little more understood, the simulator runs, and I now know — in my
bones — that "just send the bytes" is never the whole problem.</p>
<p>Also I learned to stop reading short GitHub issues at 1 AM. (I will not stop reading short
GitHub issues at 1 AM.)
